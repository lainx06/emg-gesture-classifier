\begin{abstract}
Continuous surface electromyography (sEMG) gesture classification suffers from cross-session accuracy degradation due to electrode shift and signal non-stationarity. Commodity edge hardware imposes compromises in spatial coverage, sampling rate, and signal quantization, but their relative contribution to cross-session failure is unclear. Using the open-access GRABMyo dataset (\nSubjects{} subjects), we systematically degraded an 8-channel floating-point clinical baseline into a simulated 2-channel, 10-bit, \fsCommodity{}~Hz commodity configuration across 28 days. Because all six conditions derive from identical source recordings, comparisons were exactly paired. Under Random Forest, the fixed landmark-anchored 2-channel configuration was the dominant measured bottleneck: the \texttt{[0,4]} layout, specified in advance on anatomical grounds, lowered Day-1 accuracy by \spatialDayOnePenalty{} percentage points and increased cross-session degradation by \spatialDropPenalty{} points; in a subsequent post-hoc sweep, it produced the largest penalty among four tested two-channel rotations. Reducing sampling rate and bit depth altered cross-session degradation by less than 2 percentage points over the tested ranges. A significant compounding effect was found only for Random Forest, which showed the smallest cross-session loss on 8 channels, an advantage that collapsed on 2 channels, yielding diminishing returns for complex models. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) attribution profiles showed cosine similarities of \shapCosineCommodity{} (commodity) and \shapCosineClinical{} (clinical) over 28 days despite a \rfCommDrop{}-point commodity accuracy drop, with little redistribution of input-dependent attribution under the frozen classifier. Profiling on an ATmega328P favored minimal architectures such as Linear Discriminant Analysis, for which electrode placement mattered more than channel count; windowing, not inference, dominated latency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Surface electromyography (sEMG) records muscle electrical activity through skin-mounted electrodes and is widely used for prosthetic control, rehabilitation, and wearable human--machine interfaces~\cite{ASGHARIOSKOEI2007275}. Classical time-domain feature pipelines remain attractive for embedded control because they are computationally light~\cite{204774,1206493}. Their practical reliability, however, is limited by cross-session distribution shift: after a device is removed and reapplied, electrode displacement, skin-interface changes, and other sources of non-stationarity can reduce classification accuracy~\cite{10.3389/fnbot.2018.00058,8009405}.

Low-cost acquisition introduces a second constraint. Commodity systems may use fewer electrodes, lower sampling rates, and coarser analog-to-digital conversion than research-grade hardware. Longitudinal studies generally retain multi-channel laboratory acquisition, while hardware-constrained studies commonly evaluate a single session or treat the device as an aggregate configuration~\cite{Pizzolato2017-zd,7974234}. Thus, it remains unclear which hardware compromise most strongly amplifies performance loss when a classifier is frozen and reused across days.

We address this gap with a paired ablation on the open-access GRABMyo dataset~\cite{pradhan2022grabmyo,PhysioNet-grabmyo-1.1.0}. An 8-channel, \fsClinical{}~Hz floating-point baseline is degraded independently along three axes---spatial sensing, sampling rate, and quantization---to form six conditions derived from identical source recordings. Subject-specific classifiers are trained on Day~1 and evaluated unchanged through Day~29. We additionally profile an ATmega328P implementation to connect offline accuracy with memory and timing constraints. Here, \emph{compound} denotes an interaction in which the cross-session loss under commodity constraints exceeds the corresponding loss under clinical acquisition.

The principal findings are that the predetermined landmark-anchored \texttt{[0,4]} two-channel spatial configuration produces an \spatialDayOnePenalty{}-percentage-point Day-1 penalty and \spatialDropPenalty{} additional points of Day-1-to-Day-29 degradation, whereas reducing sampling rate to \fsCommodity{}~Hz and quantizing to 10 bits changes degradation by less than 2 points over the tested ranges. Random Forest is the only tested model whose hardware-by-time interaction survives Bonferroni correction; the per-model tests do not establish that the models' interactions differ from one another. A post-hoc spatial sweep shows that the magnitude of the two-channel penalty depends strongly on placement, and embedded profiling shows that simple linear models fit comfortably on the ATmega328P while the largest Random Forest does not.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Cross-Session Myoelectric Control}
Time-domain sEMG features such as mean absolute value, waveform length, zero crossings, and related amplitude measures remain common because of their low computational cost~\cite{204774,PHINYOMARK20127420}. Cross-session degradation is nevertheless persistent and is attributed to physiological change, electrode displacement, and other sources of signal non-stationarity~\cite{10.3389/fnbot.2018.00058,8009405}. Proposed mitigations include shift-inclusive training, domain adaptation, transfer learning, and feature fusion~\cite{HARGROVE2008175,doi:10.1142/S0219843620500255,8673876,9207910}, but many require recalibration or additional computation.

\subsection{Hardware-Constrained sEMG}
Sampling rate, ADC resolution, channel configuration, and embedded inference have each been studied in myoelectric systems~\cite{5626224,verdini2026sampling,tavakoli2018doublechannel,7974234,moin2021wearable}. Device comparisons have evaluated low-cost acquisition against research-grade systems~\cite{Pizzolato2017-zd}, and benchmarks document degradation under non-ideal recording conditions~\cite{9771219}. However, these studies generally do not decompose specific hardware axes under a paired multi-session protocol. We therefore test hardware factors independently across 28 days using the same recordings, allowing the spatial, temporal, and quantization contributions to be compared without cross-dataset or cross-subject confounding.

\subsection{Electrode Placement and the Hardware--Time Gap}
Electrode shift during doffing and redonning is a major contributor to inter-session variation. Prior work has addressed this with shift-inclusive training, altered electrode geometries, and transfer-learning approaches~\cite{HARGROVE2008175,5872014,8673876}, often using dense arrays or recalibration. In parallel, minimalist systems demonstrate that strong within-session recognition is possible with few channels~\cite{tavakoli2018doublechannel}, but within-session success does not establish longitudinal robustness. The unresolved engineering question is therefore not simply whether a reduced system can classify gestures, but whether a specific reduction in sensing resources changes how rapidly a frozen classifier fails across sessions. This motivates our paired decomposition of hardware axes and the separate post-hoc analysis of channel budget versus placement.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Dataset}
This study uses the \nSubjects{}-subject GRABMyo dataset from PhysioNet (CC-BY 4.0), comprising sEMG recordings collected across three sessions (Days \sessionDays).
Electromyographic signals were acquired using a forearm setup of 16 monopolar electrodes connected to an EMGUSB2+ amplifier with a hardware gain of 500. The data were sampled at \fsClinical{} Hz and preprocessed by the dataset authors using a 10--500 Hz Butterworth bandpass filter and a 60 Hz notch filter. The sensor array consisted of two parallel circumferential rings spaced \interRingCm{}~cm apart along the forearm axis. The proximal array (Ring 1) was longitudinally positioned at one-third the forearm length from the olecranon, with electrode 1 angularly aligned to the centerline of the elbow crease. The original data collection was approved by the Office of Research Ethics of the University of Waterloo (ORE\#~31346), and all participants signed an informed consent form~\cite{pradhan2022grabmyo}. The present work is a secondary analysis of the de-identified, openly licensed recordings and involved no new participant contact.

To evaluate a fundamental control scheme spanning the anterior and posterior compartments, we extracted three classes: wrist extension (Dataset Gesture 11, remapped to Class 0), hand close (Dataset Gesture 16, remapped to Class 1), and rest (Dataset Gesture 17, remapped to Class 2). Each gesture was performed across seven trials per session, with each trial lasting 5~s. To eliminate transient onset and offset artifacts, the initial and final 0.5~s of each trial were trimmed prior to filtering. During initial data validation, we resolved two discrepancies between the repository contents and the official documentation: inconsistent session folder naming conventions and a 17th gesture class (rest) that PhysioNet version 1.1.0 includes but the original data descriptor's list of 16 gestures does not~\cite{pradhan2022grabmyo,PhysioNet-grabmyo-1.1.0}.

\subsection{Hardware Degradation Pipeline} \label{sec:hardware_degradation}
To characterize the hardware and signal-processing drivers of cross-session accuracy loss, the clinical baseline data were systematically degraded across three hardware axes: spatial resolution (channel count), temporal resolution (sample rate), and signal fidelity (bit depth). This produced six precisely controlled experimental conditions, summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. Throughout, \emph{clinical} denotes this research-grade laboratory reference acquisition, not a clinically certified device.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_pipeline.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Processing and evaluation pipeline.} Records are converted to bipolar derivations, degraded along hardware axes, causally filtered, and segmented into windowed features. The resulting feature cache enables both offline statistical evaluation and embedded profiling on an ATmega328P microcontroller. All six experimental conditions are derived from identical raw recordings.}
    \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical (Baseline):} 8~channels, \fsClinical{}~Hz sampling, floating-point precision.
    \item \textbf{Rate-only:} 8~channels, \fsCommodity{}~Hz sampling, floating-point precision.
    \item \textbf{Depth-only:} 8~channels, \fsClinical{}~Hz sampling, 10-bit quantization.
    \item \textbf{Rate-depth:} 8~channels, \fsCommodity{}~Hz sampling, 10-bit quantization.
    \item \textbf{Spatial-only:} 2~channels (F1F9 and F5F13), \fsClinical{}~Hz sampling, floating-point precision.
    \item \textbf{Commodity:} 2~channels (F1F9 and F5F13), \fsCommodity{}~Hz sampling, 10-bit quantization.
\end{itemize}

Signal degradation was applied in a strict sequential chain to reflect physical embedded processing constraints. Because all conditions were derived from identical underlying recordings, all statistical comparison in this study is strictly paired at the subject level. In accordance with the deployed signal chain, simulated quantization preceded the digital high-pass filter. The source recordings had already undergone the dataset's acquisition and preprocessing filtration. When rate degradation was required, the signal was downsampled using a rational factor ($125/256$), incorporating an anti-aliasing FIR filter. When depth degradation was required, the signal was mapped to a symmetric bipolar 10-bit resolution based on a predefined peak voltage threshold ($\Vmax$).

The $\Vmax$ thresholds were calibrated to the 99.9th percentile of the differenced, trimmed signal at the precise point in the processing chain where each respective quantizer operates. To prevent longitudinal test-set leakage, calibration was restricted to the Session 1 signal pool (including held-out Day-1 trials) without relying on class labels or cross-session data. This protocol constitutes a within-session signal-range calibration rather than strictly a training-only pipeline. Samples outside the fixed $\pm \Vmax$ range were saturated to the endpoint codes before reconstruction. Importantly, $\Vmax$ thresholds for the 8-channel depth ablations (\vmaxEightCh{} mV for \texttt{depth\_only} and \vmaxEightChResampled{}~mV for \texttt{rate\_depth}) were pooled across the entire 8-channel array. Consequently, the \texttt{depth\_only} condition used a \depthCommodityStepRatio{}$\times$ larger 10-bit quantization step than the 2-channel commodity condition ($\Vmax = $ \vmaxTwoCh{}~mV), and \texttt{rate\_depth} used a \rateDepthCommodityStepRatio{}$\times$ larger step. This asymmetry was intentionally preserved as a conservative handicap regarding finite quantization step size rather than as a claim about all ADC distortions. Across a complete audit of \quantAuditTrials{} trials, full-scale saturation affected approximately \quantSatSessionOneApprox{} of Session~1 samples---consistent with the 99.9th-percentile calibration target---and did not exceed \quantSatCrossSessionMax{} of samples across all quantized cross-session evaluations. Thus, the coarser 8-channel quantization step did not induce substantial full-scale clipping.

\subsection{Channel Selection and Bipolar Derivation}
To mimic the physical hardware of a 2-channel commodity edge sensor, monopolar recordings were converted to bipolar derivations. Corresponding electrodes at the same angular position on the two longitudinally separated rings were subtracted (e.g., F1 minus F9).

The 2-channel commodity condition isolated a single flexor-extensor pair spaced 180 degrees apart (four 45-degree steps on the circular array) to capture the anterior and posterior compartments of the forearm. Because hand close is a flexor-dominant gesture and wrist extension is extensor-dominant, this mapping provides the physiological basis for baseline separability. This landmark-anchored placement deliberately avoids per-subject spatial optimization, accurately reflecting the rigid, generalized fit of consumer armbands. To ensure strict spatial parity, the 2 commodity channels (F1F9 and F5F13) operate as a direct subset of the 8 clinical channels (Derivations 0 and 4).

In a representative trial, prior to subtraction, the paired longitudinally separated monopolar electrodes (e.g., F1 and F9) exhibited a high Pearson correlation ($r =$ \rawCorr), indicating heavy common-mode content and justifying spatial high-pass rejection via differencing. Following subtraction, the resulting bipolar channels (F1F9 and F5F13) were substantially decorrelated ($r =$ \bipolarCorr), suggesting that they carry complementary spatial information.

\subsection{Filtering and Feature Extraction}
A causal 4th-order Butterworth high-pass filter (\hpCutoff{} Hz) was applied to remove baseline wander and motion artifacts. Zero-phase filtering techniques were strictly avoided to maintain causality, ensuring the pipeline remains viable for real-time edge deployment. No additional low-pass filter was applied beyond the dataset's native 500 Hz acquisition limit. Because the source recordings were already band-limited to 500 Hz, the \fsCommodity{}~Hz condition preserves the nominal source passband at its Nyquist limit. The rate ablation therefore primarily evaluates reduced temporal sampling density rather than a broad bandwidth reduction, with practical attenuation near the upper band edge set by the anti-aliasing response of the polyphase resampler.

Following the filter chain, an additional \transientMs{} ms trim was discarded to eliminate transient IIR filter startup artifacts. This trim was floored to sample indices to maintain exact window-count symmetry across all resampling arms. Signals were then segmented into \windowMs{} ms windows with a \stepMs{} ms (50\%) step overlap, yielding exactly \nWindows{} windows per trial (totaling \nRows{} windows per condition).

Four standard time-domain features were extracted per channel: Root Mean Square (RMS), Mean Absolute Value (MAV), Waveform Length (WL), and Zero Crossings (ZC). RMS and MAV are inherently rate-independent. To ensure feature parity across the temporal degradation conditions, WL was divided by the window sample count, and ZC was divided by the window duration (yielding crossings per second). A \zcEpsilon{} $\mu$V deadzone was applied to the ZC calculation to prevent hardware noise inflation.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
The classification task was structured as a subject-dependent, longitudinal evaluation. To ensure strict training parity, models for all six degradation conditions were trained exclusively on Session 1 (Day 1), Trials 1--5. Baseline performance was established using Session 1, Trials 6--7. Cross-session evaluation used the same held-out trial indices, Trials 6--7, in both Session 2 (Day 8) and Session 3 (Day 29), with Day 29 defining the primary 28-day robustness endpoint.

To prevent leakage from the 50\% window overlap, train/test partitioning was performed at the trial level so that overlapping windows never crossed partition boundaries; inferential comparisons were subsequently aggregated at the subject level. Features were z-standardized using the training-partition mean and standard deviation, with the scaler fit exclusively on Session 1 Trials 1--5 and then applied unchanged to all held-out evaluation data. The classification pipeline evaluated five models spanning distinct model families so that model-dependent robustness and deployment tradeoffs could be compared under an otherwise identical experimental pipeline: Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA, linear discriminant), LinearSVC (margin-based), K-Nearest Neighbors ($k=5$, instance-based), Random Forest (bagged trees), and XGBoost (boosted trees). Random Forest and XGBoost both used a fixed ensemble size of 50 estimators. Hyperparameters were fixed globally and were not tuned separately by condition or subject; the same model specifications were applied unchanged across all six degradation conditions and all \nSubjects{} subjects. All stochastic algorithms (Random Forest, XGBoost, LinearSVC) and bootstrap resampling procedures utilized a fixed random seed (42) to ensure strict reproducibility. Data splits, LDA, and KNN relied entirely on deterministic operations. Software versions were recovered retrospectively from the original project virtual environment associated with the analysis: Python 3.14.2, NumPy 2.4.6, SciPy 1.18.0, scikit-learn 1.9.0, XGBoost 3.3.0, SHAP 0.52.0, WFDB 4.3.1, Pandas 3.0.3, and Matplotlib 3.11.1.

\subsection{Deployability Profiling Method}
SRAM and Flash memory footprints were profiled in \texttt{C++} to determine deployability constraints for a standard ATmega328P microcontroller (16 MHz, 2 KB SRAM, 32 KB flash, of which \appFlashLimit{} bytes remain available to the application after the 512-byte bootloader). A baseline hardware profiling sketch established the memory budget available for model parameters. Footprints were calculated for the commodity target (2 channels, 8 features, 3 classes) using exact float32 parameter counts representing the physical device payload, rather than serialized file sizes. All deployment structures include the fitted standard scaler (16 floats, 64 bytes). Footprints for fixed-architecture models (LDA, LinearSVC, KNN) were profiled using a representative subject, while tree-based ensembles (Random Forest, XGBoost) report the minimum and maximum footprint across all \nSubjects{} subjects due to their data-dependent structural variance.

Model payloads were defined per architecture: weight and bias vectors for LDA and LinearSVC, the complete training feature matrix and its corresponding labels for KNN (i.e., 555 windows $\times$ 8 features $\times$ 4 bytes, plus 555 label bytes), and explicit node array structures for tree-based models. Tree layouts assumed 9 bytes for internal nodes (1-byte feature index, 4-byte float threshold, and two 2-byte child indices), 1 byte for Random Forest leaves (class label), and 4 bytes for XGBoost leaves (float weight). These tree footprints are estimates from node counts: only the LDA implementation was compiled and executed on the device. The 1-byte Random Forest leaf assumes that each leaf stores its majority class and that the forest votes by majority, whereas scikit-learn averages the leaves' class probabilities, so a forest deployed in this form could disagree with the offline model on some windows.

To evaluate SRAM constraints, three mutually exclusive buffer architectures were analyzed: a naive float32 window (1,600 bytes), an \texttt{int16} window (800 bytes), and a streaming accumulator architecture whose accumulator, two rotating \subBlockBytes{}-byte sub-blocks per channel, measured \accumBytesMeasured{} bytes on the target (\texttt{sizeof}). The streaming design accumulates features over \stepMs{} ms sub-blocks without storing the raw window, and is the architecture carried through to the verified implementation in Section~\ref{sec:embedded_verification}.

Microsecond-resolution execution timing was captured to measure ADC, filtering, and inference latencies against a 1 kHz sampling deadline. Per-stage latencies were measured using a loop-and-divide methodology, with the empty-loop iteration overhead subtracted from each per-iteration average; the single-shot \texttt{micros()} call overhead was measured but not subtracted, as it contributes less than 0.01~$\mu$s per iteration over 1,000 iterations. To prevent the compiler's optimizer from silently deleting unconsumed arithmetic during benchmarking, a \texttt{volatile} sink was utilized. A worst-case tick was estimated separately as the maximum duration over 1,000 individually timed window-boundary ticks, each performing ADC reads, filtering, feature finalization, and LDA scoring; it is the largest observed value in this benchmark, not a guaranteed bound. These latencies were obtained from a dedicated profiling sketch that reproduces the computational workload of the deployed pipeline, using the same filter order, feature set, and window structure, but implemented with direct-form biquad sections and a single running accumulator. It is therefore distinct from the verified firmware described in Section~\ref{sec:embedded_verification}, which uses transposed direct form II and rotating self-contained sub-blocks. The reported latencies are representative of the per-stage computational cost rather than measurements taken on the verified implementation.

\subsection{Embedded Firmware Execution Verification} \label{sec:embedded_verification}
To verify numerical equivalence between the deployed implementation and the offline analysis on a held-out deployment test partition, we performed a three-level numerical comparison. Level A is the analysis pipeline described above, using SciPy and scikit-learn in double precision. Level B is an explicit single-precision reference implemented in Python that mirrors the operation order of the embedded code: transposed direct form II filtering applied sample by sample, multiplication by a precomputed reciprocal in place of division during standardization, and ordered accumulation of the discriminant dot product. Level C is the physical ATmega328P executing replayed quantized ADC codes. The A-to-B comparison isolates the numerical consequence of these deployment choices; the B-to-C comparison isolates firmware execution correctness from ADC-code reconstruction onward.

Because the streaming architecture processes each sample once and cannot revisit earlier data, features are accumulated over \stepMs{} ms sub-blocks that are combined pairwise into \windowMs{} ms windows. Each sub-block retains only statistics computed from its own samples. The single sample-to-sample difference spanning the boundary between two adjacent sub-blocks belongs to neither and is added when the pair is joined; retaining it inside either block would double count it, because each sub-block contributes to two consecutive overlapping windows. This reformulation was verified against the direct windowed implementation on 3,500 synthetic cases constructed to exercise the zero-crossing deadzone and sign conventions at sub-block boundaries, and on real trials, with agreement to machine precision.

Quantized 10-bit codes were streamed to the microcontroller over a framed serial protocol with per-chunk acknowledgement, so transfer rate could not perturb the arithmetic. A CRC-16 computed over the received bytes confirmed that codes arrived intact, distinguishing transport faults from implementation error. Filter and accumulator state were reset at each trial boundary, matching the reference. The device returned raw and standardized features, discriminant scores, and predicted classes for every window; a separate diagnostic mode additionally returned every filtered sample for one trial.

Acceptance criteria were fixed before any hardware result was observed: exact agreement for ADC codes, zero-crossing counts, window counts, and predictions; filter output within 0.5 ADC least significant bits; standardized features within $10^{-3}$ training standard deviations; and discriminant scores within a relative tolerance of $10^{-4}$ and an absolute tolerance of $10^{-5}$. Because a per-score error of magnitude $E$ can lower the winning score and raise the runner-up simultaneously, the observed margin shrinks by $2E$; we therefore adopted $2E < M$, for minimum decision margin $M$, as a sufficient worst-case condition for preserving the predicted class. It is not a necessary condition, and predictions may agree when it fails. A comparison of the continuous raw features was added after the initial hardware results were observed; it is reported as a post-hoc diagnostic and did not form part of the acceptance criteria.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
To distinguish analyses specified before subsequent evaluation from later exploratory analyses, the statistical analysis plan was formally locked after an initial observation of the primary main effects.

Four planned paired Wilcoxon contrasts characterized the LDA hardware-by-session cell means: clinical versus commodity on Day 1, clinical versus commodity on Day 29, Day 1 versus Day 29 under clinical acquisition, and Day 1 versus Day 29 under commodity constraints. A Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of $\alpha = 0.0125$ was applied.

The primary analysis evaluates, separately for each model, the hardware-by-time interaction in the cross-session drop (the $2\times2$ interaction). The performance of the clinical baseline vs. commodity hardware across Sessions 1 and 3 was evaluated for all five model architectures. Significance was tested using a two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Confidence intervals (95\%) for the interaction were computed using a percentile bootstrap over 10,000 resamples. To correct for multiple comparisons across the five models, a Bonferroni threshold of $\alpha = \bonferroniAlpha$ was applied.

A secondary descriptive analysis evaluated this cross-session drop normalized against available performance headroom (Day 1 accuracy minus a 33.3\% theoretical chance floor). To prevent division by near-zero, a union exclusion mask was applied: any subject scoring $\le$ 40\% on Day 1 under any model in either condition was dropped for this specific endpoint, excluding 2 of the \nSubjects{} subjects and leaving $n=41$. The mask applies only to this descriptive headroom-normalized endpoint; the primary analyses retain all \nSubjects{} subjects. The 40\% threshold was chosen to place the denominator at least 6.7 percentage points above the 33.3\% chance floor; it is a pragmatic bound rather than a principled one, and it applies only to this secondary endpoint. Unlike the primary metric, this descriptive endpoint was not subject to multiplicity correction.

The third analysis, the hardware ablation, compares the contributions of the tested hardware degradation conditions to the performance penalty. The independent endpoints are the difference in cross-session degradation (Day 1 minus Day 29 accuracy) and the difference in Day 1 baseline accuracy between the clinical baseline and each of the five degradation variants. To bound the exact effect sizes without discarding magnitude, 95\% confidence intervals for the paired per-subject differences were calculated using the Bias-Corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap method. No multiplicity correction was applied to the ablation intervals because this analysis is estimation-focused. The intervals are interpreted individually and descriptively and should not be treated as simultaneous family-wise bounds.

\subsection{Feature Attribution Method}
To characterize feature-attribution behavior relevant to the spatial-redundancy hypothesis, Random Forest feature attribution was analyzed across all six degradation conditions using TreeExplainer (SHAP)~\cite{lundberg2017unifiedapproachinterpretingmodel,Lundberg2020trees}. The analysis isolated the eight features originating from the physical locations shared between devices (F1F9 and F5F13) to measure two metrics specified in the locked analysis plan between Day 1 and Day 29: Attribution Migration (the fraction of total model decision weight placed on the shared channels) and Profile Shift (the cosine distance of the normalized 8-feature attribution profile).

To isolate true longitudinal shift from dimensionality noise, cross-session profile shifts were normalized against an empirical within-day baseline. The baseline averaged within-day noise (Day 1 Trial 6 vs. Trial 7, and Day 29 Trial 6 vs. Trial 7), while the longitudinal shift averaged cross-day matched trials (T6 vs. T6, and T7 vs. T7). Each profile pooled one repetition (trial 6 or 7) of each of the three gestures, giving 111 windows per profile, so window counts were matched across the four profiles. The four 8-channel conditions served as a falsification control: the analysis plan stated that if channel count drove the effect, the 8-channel models would show significantly less profile shift than the 2-channel models, and that clinical attribution migration would shift, in either direction, as the shared features' discriminative value changed on Day 29 against the frozen training-time splits. Day-1 and Day-29 attribution migration were compared with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Baseline-normalized profile shift was compared between each 8-channel condition and each 2-channel condition with paired Wilcoxon tests, giving eight comparisons evaluated against a Bonferroni threshold of $\alpha = 0.05/8 = 0.0063$.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Baseline and Hardware--Session Effects}
The clinical within-session accuracy was 96.2\% for LDA and 97.2\% for Random Forest. Under LDA, clinical accuracy changed from 96.2\% on Day~1 to 82.5\% on Day~29, while commodity accuracy changed from 86.1\% to 71.4\%. All four planned LDA hardware/session contrasts were significant after Bonferroni correction ($p\leq1.7\times10^{-3}$ for the least significant contrast).

By Day~29, Random Forest errors were concentrated in Extension$\leftrightarrow$Close confusion under both acquisition conditions (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}). Commodity Rest recall also declined substantially by Day~29, indicating that cross-session loss was not limited to confusion between the two active gestures.

The Random-Forest-versus-LDA robustness advantage also increased with channel budget, from $-1.05$ points at two channels to $+8.29$ at eight, consistent with---but not proving---a training-time spatial-redundancy mechanism. Part of this trend reflects LDA degrading more with eight channels than with several two-channel layouts rather than Random Forest improving. Alternative explanations include model capacity, bias--variance behavior (for example, less stable covariance estimates as LDA's input dimension grows), and differing ability to exploit feature interactions.

\subsection{Attribution Analysis}
Feature-attribution migration in the clinical condition changed from \shapMigrationDayOne{} to \shapMigrationDayTwentyNine{} ($p=\shapMigrationP{}$). Baseline-normalized profile shifts were directionally smaller for all four 8-channel conditions than for the two 2-channel conditions, but none survived multiplicity correction. The raw Day-1-to-Day-29 cosine similarities remained high (\shapCosineCommodity{} commodity; \shapCosineClinical{} clinical) despite a \rfCommDrop{}-point commodity accuracy drop. Thus, error increased without a large redistribution of input-dependent attribution under the frozen classifier; this does not identify the physical cause of the shift.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Spatial Sensing Is the Dominant Tested Hardware Constraint}
The paired ablation establishes a clear hierarchy within the tested hardware ranges. Reducing rate to \fsCommodity{}~Hz or quantizing to 10 bits produced little change in either Day-1 performance or cross-session degradation, whereas the fixed two-channel spatial configuration substantially affected both. The post-hoc sweep refines rather than overturns this result: two-channel performance depends strongly on placement, and the primary \texttt{[0,4]} layout was toward the pessimistic end of the tested rotations. Accordingly, the engineering conclusion is not that every two-channel system incurs an 11-point penalty, but that aggressive loss of spatial coverage can dominate the tested rate and resolution compromises and becomes highly placement-sensitive. The ordering of coverage and placement also depended on the model: for LDA, a well-placed two-channel layout degraded less across sessions than the eight-channel model, so placement, not channel count alone, should guide the design of simple embedded classifiers.

These findings complement prior device and parameter studies. Pizzolato et al. reported comparable performance between commodity and research-grade systems~\cite{Pizzolato2017-zd}, while Verdini et al. found a benefit of 1000 over 500~Hz on a physical wearable system~\cite{verdini2026sampling}. Our near-null \fsClinical{}-to-\fsCommodity{}~Hz result is therefore bounded to this higher-rate, already band-limited regime and should not be extrapolated to lower-rate devices. The paired design instead highlights spatial sensing as the binding constraint in the tested configuration.

\subsection{Model Dependence and Attribution}
Random Forest was the only model whose hardware-by-time interaction survived correction. One candidate explanation is training-time spatial redundancy: with eight channels, tree splits can be distributed across more spatial inputs, whereas a two-channel constraint removes that redundancy. The spatial sweep is consistent with this interpretation, because the ensemble's robustness advantage over LDA emerged as channel budget increased, but part of that trend comes from LDA degrading more with eight channels than with several two-channel layouts. The experiment does not isolate redundancy from model capacity or feature-interaction effects, so this remains a mechanistic hypothesis rather than a demonstrated cause.

The SHAP analysis similarly constrains interpretation. Attribution profiles remained highly similar across sessions even as accuracy declined, indicating that the frozen model did not dramatically redistribute input-dependent attribution. This is consistent with shifted feature values crossing fixed decision boundaries, but attribution stability alone does not reveal the underlying physiological or electrode-interface mechanism.

\subsection{Timescale, Embedded Implications, and Future Work}
Most Random Forest loss appeared by Day~8 and changed little thereafter. This pattern is consistent with either a perturbation associated with removal/reapplication or drift that saturates within the first week, but three sessions cannot distinguish those mechanisms. Denser measurements early in deployment, or controlled repeated reapplication within a day, are needed before recommending a recalibration schedule.

The embedded results show that constrained sensing can compress the accuracy benefit of complex models while leaving substantial memory differences. LDA fits easily on the ATmega328P; the largest Random Forest does not. Computation contributes under 2~ms to an average controller delay of approximately 152~ms, which is set by the \windowMs{}-ms window and \stepMs{}-ms increment. Shorter windows or increments, rather than faster inference, are therefore required to reduce controller delay.

The confusion matrices also show a practical failure mode: under commodity constraints, Rest recall falls substantially by Day~29. Because the analysis reports overlapping windows rather than independent control events, these errors cannot be converted directly into false-activation frequency. Nonetheless, they motivate future evaluation with temporal smoothing and event-level control metrics, where any accuracy benefit must be balanced against additional latency.

The next experimental step is physical acquisition. A low-cost multi-channel sEMG front end should test repeated sensor removal/reapplication, controlled placement perturbations, shorter windows, and denser early-session sampling. Such a system would determine whether the spatial-coverage hierarchy observed in controlled replay persists when electrode contact, analog noise, gain, ADC utilization, and real-time scheduling are introduced together.

\section{Limitations}
The task contains only three classes and uses able-bodied participants from one dataset, so absolute accuracies and effect magnitudes should not be generalized to larger gesture sets or residual-limb EMG. The primary two-channel layout was fixed on anatomical grounds, but the exploratory sweep shows substantial placement sensitivity; the primary \texttt{[0,4]} estimates therefore describe that layout rather than two-channel sensing universally, and the finding that coverage outweighs placement held for Random Forest but not for LDA. The sweep tests only regular rotations and one intermediate four-channel budget, and nominal channel indices were retained across sessions rather than experimentally imposing controlled electrode displacement.

The rate and depth ablations are also conditional on the source recordings. GRABMyo was already band-limited to 500~Hz, so the \fsCommodity{}~Hz condition does not test substantial bandwidth removal and should not be extrapolated to much lower-rate devices. Quantization used a Session-1-derived 99.9th-percentile full-scale range and ideal source notch filtering; a physical front end with different gain, headroom, interference, or ADC utilization may exhibit greater loss. The pooled 8-channel thresholds also produced larger quantization steps than the two-channel condition, so the depth result is conservative only with respect to finite step size, not every analog-front-end distortion. The calibration used all Session-1 signals, including held-out within-day trials, although cross-session data remained isolated; likewise, the 20~$\mu$V zero-crossing deadzone was chosen after inspecting Session-1 recordings from one subject, including its held-out trials.

Embedded verification used replayed ADC codes for one subject and therefore validates numerical execution rather than electrodes, analog acquisition, or continuous real-time sampling. The timing profile was measured in a representative profiling sketch rather than the numerically verified firmware. Continuous \fsCommodity{}~Hz scheduling was not implemented. Finally, confidence intervals for some model interactions were wide, the analysis plan was locked after initial observation rather than preregistered, and the feature/model scope was limited to classical time-domain pipelines. These constraints motivate replication with physical acquisition, more subjects/tasks, denser spatial layouts, shorter windows, and alternative model families.

\section{Conclusion}
Across paired 28-day sEMG evaluations, the tested spatial sensing constraint produced a substantially larger performance penalty than reducing sampling rate from \fsClinical{} to \fsCommodity{}~Hz or quantizing to 10 bits. The fixed \texttt{[0,4]} two-channel layout reduced Day-1 accuracy by \spatialDayOnePenalty{} points and added \spatialDropPenalty{} points of cross-session degradation; a post-hoc sweep showed that the magnitude depends strongly on placement. A significant hardware-by-time interaction was found only for Random Forest, and the accuracy advantage of complex models compressed under constrained sensing. ATmega328P playback reproduced all \nVerifyWindows{} tested predictions, while memory and timing profiling favored simple architectures and showed that continuous 1-kHz scheduling remains unresolved. Within the tested ranges, robust edge myoelectric design should prioritize electrode placement and spatial coverage before additional sampling precision or model complexity; for the simple linear models that fit on the target microcontroller, placement mattered more than channel count.
